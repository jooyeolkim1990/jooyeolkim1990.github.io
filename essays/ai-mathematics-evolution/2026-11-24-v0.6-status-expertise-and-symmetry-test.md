# v0.6 — Status, Expertise, and the Symmetry Test

**Line of thought developed:** 2026-11-24  
**Committed:** 2026-11-24  
**Status:** Current conversation-derived note.

The Riemann-hypothesis episode sharpened a distinction that had not been explicit in the earlier versions: expertise and symbolic authority are not the same thing.

Levent Alpöge is clearly a professional mathematician with elite training and strong credentials. But he does not occupy the same symbolic position as a figure such as Terence Tao, whose name itself carries extraordinary institutional and reputational weight.

That difference creates a useful test.

## Expertise is not the same as symbolic authority

A researcher can be fully qualified to do mathematics without possessing enough accumulated prestige to change how others react to the same work.

This suggests a distinction between a **credential floor** and a **symbolic ceiling**.

Crossing the credential floor means that a person is difficult to dismiss as an outsider who does not belong in the field.

Reaching the symbolic ceiling means something stronger: the person's reputation may itself alter the burden of proof, the tone of criticism, or the willingness of others to assume that an unfamiliar method must contain something worth understanding.

The question is therefore not merely whether a person is a mathematician.

It is whether the person's existing status changes how the community applies its own principles.

## The symmetry test

A clean counterfactual is possible.

Hold constant:

- the same AI system;
- the same amount of AI contribution;
- the same proof object;
- the same level of human understanding;
- the same verification status;
- the same release process.

Then change only the identity and status of the mathematician presenting the result.

If the community's judgment changes substantially when the author changes from a relatively less powerful mathematician to a figure with exceptional symbolic authority, that would suggest that the dispute is not governed only by methodological principles.

The sharp version of the question is:

> **If Terence Tao had produced the same proof with the same AI, would the mathematical community have applied the same ethical and epistemic standards in the same way?**

This is not a prediction that the answer must be no.

It is a symmetry test.

## A result that can fail without weakening the argument

The value of the test is that it does not require the community to behave badly.

If high-status insiders receive softer treatment under otherwise identical conditions, that would be evidence that symbolic authority affects the application of norms.

If high-status insiders are criticized under the same standards, then the norms are more robust and status-neutral than suspected.

If the most prominent mathematicians already insist on the same standards regardless of status, there is little to criticize on this dimension.

The point is not to prove snobbery in advance.

The point is to make status dependence observable.

## Discovery rights and validation rights

The episode also sharpened another distinction.

Mathematicians have strong claims to expertise in verification, interpretation, historical context, and the assessment of mathematical significance.

That does not automatically imply an exclusive right to perform exploration or first discovery.

This distinction matters more now because the current pattern is not simply "AI versus mathematicians."

It is increasingly:

**mathematician + AI + formal verification + institutional gatekeepers.**

Once these elements separate, the old bundle begins to come apart.

The same professional community no longer necessarily controls:

- who can explore;
- who can discover;
- who can verify;
- who can interpret;
- who can confer recognition.

Expertise may justify a strong role in some of these stages without establishing ownership over all of them.

## The new institutional question

The Riemann-hypothesis episode therefore makes the authority problem more concrete.

The central issue is no longer only whether AI-generated mathematics is valid or understandable.

It is also whether:

> **the standards applied to AI-assisted mathematics remain stable when the status of the human mathematician changes.**

This turns symbolic capital from a background feature of academic life into something that can, at least in principle, be tested against the community's stated norms.

The broader question remains the same:

AI companies should not be allowed to manufacture authority merely by choosing favored experts.

But existing academic authority should not be treated as a neutral background condition either.

If authority affects who is heard, who is doubted, who is presumed competent, and how severely a method is criticized, then authority itself is part of the governance problem.
