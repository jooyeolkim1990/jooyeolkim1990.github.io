# v0.4 — Authority, Control, and the Remote Control

**Line of thought developed by:** 2026-10-31  
**Retrospectively reconstructed and committed:** 2026-11-24  
**Status:** Private-conversation reconstruction, not contemporaneous public publication.

By late October, the controversy had moved beyond the question of whether AI should solve open problems.

AGMAI's discussion of company-designated mathematicians made another layer visible: **who gets to allocate mathematical authority**.

## The procedural concern is real

If a company gives an unpublished AI result to mathematicians of its own choosing, those people receive privileged access.

They may become the first interpreters, validators, or public faces associated with the result.

A prominent mathematician's reputation can then function as a form of borrowed credibility for the company.

That creates genuine conflicts.

A strong procedural response is possible: disclose results through channels not controlled by the company, provide sufficient technical information for independent verification, and allow the research community to organize interpretation more autonomously.

## The symmetry problem

But a further question follows.

If a company selecting particular mathematicians is an exercise of power, why should the established academic allocation of authority be treated as politically neutral?

The two systems are not identical.

Corporate privileged access to unpublished work has features that ordinary academic evaluation does not.

Still, the academic system also allocates authority through prizes, elite appointments, invitations, institutional prestige, recommendations, networks, students, funding, and agenda-setting power.

Authority compounds.

Prestige produces visibility; visibility produces opportunities; opportunities produce further prestige and resources.

This is structurally similar to a broader concern about cumulative authority: an earlier achievement can become a credential that attracts new attention, capital, people, and institutional deference long after the original achievement.

The relevant question therefore became:

> Who gets to allocate mathematical authority?

## The remote-control metaphor

Before AI, mathematicians largely held the controls over several connected stages:

- what problems mattered;
- who discovered important results;
- who verified them;
- who interpreted them;
- who received prestige;
- who gained the standing to shape the next agenda.

AI companies can now intervene in some of those stages directly.

They can fund large-scale search, choose targets, generate results, select experts, control release timing, and attract enormous public attention.

Some of the buttons on the remote control have moved into another hand.

This does not establish that mathematicians who resist the change are consciously defending personal status. Motive should not be invented.

The structural conflict exists without that claim.

The controversy increasingly concerns the redistribution of control over discovery, verification, interpretation, visibility, and prestige.

## Current distinction at this stage

The strongest position was no longer simply "mathematicians should let AI solve problems."

It became:

> The mathematical community has legitimate procedural standing over the research norms it has built. But if it objects to corporate allocation of authority, its own allocation of authority must also remain open to scrutiny.

Corporate power is power.

Academic power is also power.

Neither side owns mathematics.
