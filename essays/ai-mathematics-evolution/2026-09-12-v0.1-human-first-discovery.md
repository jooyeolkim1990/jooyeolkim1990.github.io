# v0.1 — Human First Discovery, Not Mathematics

**Line of thought developed by:** 2026-09-12  
**Retrospectively reconstructed and committed:** 2026-11-24  
**Status:** Private-conversation reconstruction, not contemporaneous public publication.

The initial argument began as a response to the September 2026 statement by Terence Tao and other Fields Medalists warning that AI companies' race to solve major open mathematical problems could damage the "fertile ground" from which mathematics develops.

The strongest part of that warning was easy to accept. Famous open problems have historically generated much more than final answers. Failed approaches, partial results, new concepts, new proof techniques, and unexpected connections often emerge during long attempts to solve them.

The weak step was the inference that humans must therefore preserve those problems as unsolved territory for human exploration.

## Search costs are often compressed

Scholarship routinely advances by allowing later generations to avoid repeating earlier search costs.

Alchemy produced techniques and observations that later mattered to chemistry. Modern chemistry students do not reproduce centuries of alchemical trial and error before receiving the surviving knowledge.

The same applies to calculation and writing. Friction can accompany thought without being a necessary condition for thought. Slow handwriting may encourage reflection; that does not imply that serious thinking requires preserving the physical cost of handwriting.

AI extends this old pattern into a more sensitive domain because the outsourced activity now includes parts of problem decomposition, hypothesis generation, search, and proof construction.

That makes the transition important. It does not by itself make the old search cost something that must be preserved.

## First discovery and understanding are different

An AI system solving a problem first removes one opportunity with certainty: a human can no longer become the first solver of that problem.

That loss is real. In mathematics, first solution can bring prestige, prizes, invitations, appointments, and permanent historical recognition.

But priority is not identical to understanding.

Humans can still reconstruct a proof, simplify it, criticize it, generalize it, weaken assumptions, find a different proof, connect the result to another field, or use it to formulate new questions.

The earliest concise formulation of the thesis was therefore:

> AI may be threatening a particular culture of mathematical discovery — especially scarce human first discovery on pre-certified prestige problems — rather than mathematics itself.

## Go and exploration

AlphaGo provided a useful analogy.

Once machines became stronger than the best human Go players, humans lost their monopoly on finding the strongest moves first. Human Go did not disappear. Players studied AI moves, changed established judgments, and developed new knowledge around them.

Geographic exploration suggested the same distinction. The first-arrival slot at the South Pole closed permanently once it was taken. Antarctic inquiry did not end.

A first-discovery frontier can close without inquiry itself closing.

## Strongest objection at this stage

The strongest objection was not prestige but human capability.

If AI performs most frontier search, humans may become worse at navigating genuinely unknown mathematical territory independently.

That concern deserves separate treatment.

But even if independent research capacity should be preserved, it does not follow that famous named problems must remain unsolved so that humans can train on them.

The object worth preserving would be the capacity, not necessarily the unsolved status of a specific prestige target.
