# v0.2 — The Treasure Hunt and Community Norms

**Line of thought developed by:** 2026-09-13  
**Retrospectively reconstructed and committed:** 2026-11-24  
**Status:** Private-conversation reconstruction, not contemporaneous public publication.

The argument became more sympathetic to the mathematicians once the conflict was reframed as a question of entering a research environment built by a community.

## The treasure-hunt analogy

Imagine a group that has spent years searching a mountain for treasure.

Some study old maps. Some draw possible routes in the dirt. Some dig by hand. Failed locations are recorded. Partial clues become shared knowledge. The activity develops its own norms and culture.

Then an uninvited outsider arrives with a metal detector, sweeps the ground, hears a beep, digs up the treasure, and effectively says:

> I win. You lose.

The resentment is understandable.

It becomes even more understandable if the metal detector was built using the maps, records, methods, and failed searches accumulated by the existing community, while the company later presents the discovery mainly as evidence of the detector's superiority.

## A stronger case for respecting mathematicians

This version therefore granted AI companies stronger obligations than the initial argument did.

A company entering an established research environment has reasons to respect:

- prior work and attribution;
- human and AI contribution boundaries;
- verification;
- priority;
- responsible release;
- the difference between scholarly communication and capability marketing.

The key distinction became:

> The fact that an open problem is not owned by mathematicians does not mean that an AI company enters a norm-free space.

A research community may have legitimate procedural standing without owning the underlying mathematical truth.

## Respect does not imply reservation

The next distinction followed immediately.

A community can define the rules of a competition it creates. It can create human-only contests, AI-only challenges, training environments, or research programs with explicit participation rules.

That is a reservation of participation.

It is different from reserving knowledge itself.

The claim "this competition is for humans" is easy to understand.

The claim "this mathematical statement remains unsolved by humans, therefore an AI should not solve it first" requires a different justification.

The treasure-hunt community may reasonably demand that an outsider respect its records and rules.

That does not automatically give the group an exclusive right to every treasure in the mountain.
