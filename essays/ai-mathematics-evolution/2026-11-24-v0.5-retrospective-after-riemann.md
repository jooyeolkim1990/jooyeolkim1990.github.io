# v0.5 — Retrospective After the Riemann-Hypothesis Episode

**Current retrospective date:** 2026-11-24  
**Committed:** 2026-11-24  
**Status:** Current note, not a claim of earlier public priority.

By November, developments in AI mathematics had made several distinctions from the earlier argument more concrete.

The public controversy was no longer only about hypothetical future AI systems or one isolated famous problem.

Claims around major open problems, large batches of machine-generated mathematics, formal verification, human interpretation, and the governance of release had all become active parts of the debate.

## Solution, understanding, and integration are separate stages

One of the earliest objections to the "AI will solve mathematics" narrative was that a machine might produce formally valid mathematics that humans do not yet understand.

That possibility does not collapse the categories.

A result can be:

1. produced;
2. formally checked or otherwise verified;
3. understood conceptually by humans;
4. integrated into the broader mathematical literature and training culture.

These stages need not occur simultaneously.

If AI reaches stage 1 or 2 before humans reach stage 3, the remaining task is not empty.

Human interpretation, reconstruction, simplification, explanation, generalization, and evaluation become separate research work.

This supports the earlier distinction between first discovery and understanding.

## The controversy has become institutional

At the same time, the governance dispute has sharpened.

The question is no longer only whether an AI system can solve an open problem.

It is also:

- who chooses which problems receive massive computational attention;
- who gets privileged access to results;
- who verifies them;
- who interprets them;
- who controls release;
- who receives credit;
- who gains authority from the episode.

The "remote control" metaphor therefore remains useful.

AI is redistributing intellectual labor, but also the institutional controls around that labor.

## Current core position

The mathematical community has strong reasons to demand respect for attribution, verification, priority, responsible release, and independent interpretation.

AI companies should not treat the scholarly infrastructure they depend on as free raw material for capability marketing.

But a research community's legitimate procedural standing does not establish ownership of the unknown.

Human-only spaces can be created where the human process itself is worth preserving.

That is different from declaring undiscovered mathematical truth reserved for human first discovery.

The current position is therefore:

> **Respect for a research community is not a right to keep knowledge undiscovered.**

And:

> **If corporate allocation of mathematical authority is a power problem, the existing academic allocation of mathematical authority must also be treated as a power structure rather than a natural background condition.**

AI companies do not own mathematics.

The mathematical establishment does not own mathematics either.

The live question is how discovery, verification, interpretation, and authority should be governed when neither side can plausibly claim exclusive control.
