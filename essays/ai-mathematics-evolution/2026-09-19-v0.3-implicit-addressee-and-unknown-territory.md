# v0.3 — The Implicit Addressee of an Open Problem

**Line of thought developed by:** 2026-09-19  
**Retrospectively reconstructed and committed:** 2026-11-24  
**Status:** Private-conversation reconstruction, not contemporaneous public publication.

By this stage, the central question had shifted.

The interesting issue was no longer only whether AI would reduce the stock of famous unsolved problems.

It was: **Who was an open problem implicitly addressed to?**

## Two propositions that had been collapsed

Before capable AI, these two statements could easily look identical:

> Humanity does not yet know the answer.

and

> A human mathematician will eventually discover the answer.

There was little reason to separate them because there was no credible non-human solver.

AI exposed the hidden assumption.

The first statement describes a state of knowledge.

The second contains a rule about who should change that state first.

An open problem does not logically contain that rule.

## From named problems to the unknown itself

The argument becomes harder to defend if human priority is extended beyond famous named problems.

Mathematics contains territory that humans have not yet formulated into recognizable problems.

We may not know which structures exist, which propositions are important, or even what questions should be asked.

Suppose an AI system discovers a theorem in such a region and only afterward do human mathematicians recognize that the result opens an important area.

It would be difficult to claim retrospectively that humans had possessed a prior right to discover it first.

They did not know what was there.

They had not formulated the problem.

They had not identified the area as important.

This produced the strongest line in this stage of the argument:

> **You cannot reserve for yourself what you do not yet know exists.**

## Procedural standing, not ownership of the unknown

The mathematical community can still make strong claims about the institutions it has built.

It can demand proper attribution, transparent verification, responsible release, and respect for existing researchers.

It can create protected human-only environments where the process itself is the point.

But these claims concern research practice and institutional procedure.

They do not establish a general human priority right over unknown mathematical truth.

The emerging boundary was:

> A community can govern a research practice. It cannot automatically claim priority over an unknown territory whose contents it cannot yet identify.
